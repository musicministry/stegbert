Organ Replacement Project

A proposal

BLUF

The organ at St. Egbert’s is now more than 30 years old, making it vulnerable to part failures and expensive repairs. The parish should begin planning and raising money to replace it within the next few years, aiming to do so before it stops working to a degree that it is unfeasible to repair. Estimated cost of the project ranges from $261,000 to $362,000 (all inclusive) in 2025, with prices increasing roughly 16% each year.

Overview

The current organ at St. Egbert’s is a two manual (keyboard) Allen MDS-6 AK digital computer organ installed in September 1994. While Allen advertises indefinite support and repair of any organ they have ever sold, the reality is that, as instruments age, so too does the technology inside and parts become increasingly difficult to obtain as old technology is replaced by newer state-of-the-art counterparts. Eventually, old parts are no longer made, and once that happens, the only way to get replacement parts is to buy one used off a similar model organ that has been turned in for parts.

At 31 years old, St. Egbert’s is at risk of confronting this if the sound module (i.e., the motherboard or “guts” of the organ) dies. If this were to happen, Allen would need to replace it with the same part, but off an equally old organ that was turned in to be used for parts. For an estimated cost of around $15-20k (as of 2026), the church would end up replacing a 30+ year old sound module with another 30+ year old module, with no way of knowing how long it will last, as it would be equally close to its end-of-life as the original one.

Digital organs are budget organs for churches that are unable to afford full pipe organs. Pipe organs, which can cost more than 10x as much as a comparably sized digital organ, are generally custom-built for the room in which they’re installed, making it essentially a part of the room or building. Properly maintained, a pipe organ will outlive the building. Digital organs, on the other hand, are more like an electronic appliance with an unavoidable shelf life. While there are digital organs that are more than 50 years old still in use, they are not the norm and have very likely undergone expensive repairs over their lifetime. On average, churches should expect to replace a digital organ ever 30-40 years.

Replacing a digital organ can present exciting opportunities for a parish, not the least of which is a significant upgrade in audio sampling technology. As St. Egbert’s enters this last quarter of the average digital organ’s life, the parish would greatly benefit from proactively fundraising now so that it can comfortably replace it with something fitting for the church before the congregation finds itself desperately needing to quickly raise money for a dead organ. This project has been prompted by a couple recent repairs that have been needed in the last two years: a power supply in 2024 and an audio control switch in 2025. At risk of future failure are the amplifiers, speakers, sound module, and other controls, to name a few.

Problems with the existing organ

1. Parts failure due to age

At 31 years old (as of early 2026), the current organ has reached the age at which most digital organs need to be replaced unless the church wants to spend money on keeping old technology running. In Advent 2024, the power supply for the organ failed spectacularly in the middle of a Sunday mass. When this happened, every note of every stop on the organ started sounding continuously at full volume, a consequence of improperly regulated power being received by the sound board. All that could be done was switch the organ off, rendering it unusable right before Christmas. The service call plus parts and labor cost a few thousand dollars. Fortunately, power supplies are not specialized parts, so a newer model was able to be installed.

Roughly a year later, an audio control switch failed, rendering half of the organ unusable. Because of the way old organs were designed, “half of the organ” meant that seemingly random stops across all divisions and within families of stops were affected. For example, in the Great division, the 8’ principal stop became inoperable while the 4’ principal stop still worked, and the 8’ flute stop in the Great division worked but the 4’ flute did not. The situation was similar in the Swell and Pedal divisions as well. This made it impossible to properly register the instrument for hymn singing and other musical needs. Again, this service call plus parts and labor was around $2000.

Fortunately, neither of these parts were specialized parts. A newer model power supply was installed (the old one was not made anymore) and the switch, while not used on new organs anymore, was used for a sufficiently long period of time that they can still be acquired. Consequently, these two service calls cost less than $10k. Nevertheless, there are dozens of other switches in the organ, and service calls to replace them one at a time as they fail will add up. Even more notable is what has not yet failed, but could at any time. The most expensive part would be the sound module, the motherboard or “guts” of the organ. The board is no longer made, so any replacement would have to be used. The service technician estimated this repair, should be it be needed, to be tens of thousands of dollars – for another 30+ year old part that could itself fail at any time. Other parts at risk of failing include the speakers (i.e. dried out and blown speaker cones), amplifiers (one for each of the two channels), the digital transposer, and the expression pedal (volume control), to name a few.

2. Outdated audio technology

As the name implies, digital computer organs use onboard computers to replicate organ stops. Computers – and all digital technology – have come a long way in 30+ years. Built and installed in 1994, the current organ uses technology from the late 1980s and early 1990s. Each stop is digitally synthesized, meaning the sound is digitally imitated to the best of the ability of the technology at the time. While state-of-the-art in 1994, by modern standards the sound generated by this organ is underwhelming, unconvincing, and uninspiring. Nowadays, the best pipe organs around the world are carefully recorded using state-of-the-art recording equipment and these recordings are essentially replicated or played back when keys are played, making the sound of new digital instruments indistinguishable from real pipe organs.

Interestingly, the majority of churches that replace old digital organs with new modern instruments experience notable increases in congregational singing and participation in music. The reason can likely be attributed to the same reason the Catholic church holds the pipe organ in the highest esteem above all other instruments: its unique ability to imitate the human voice. Both organs and the human voice are wind instruments whereby sound is created by moving air through a pipe. It turns out that the human ear is keenly sensitive to the authenticity of such sound. The authenticity of modern audio sampling and reproduction enhances our experience of the music it produces and inspires us to sing.

3. Organ is too small for liturgical and music ministry needs

The current organ is one of the smallest church organs Allen made at the time. The fact that it has an internal speaker in the console suggests that this model was intended for small churches, chapels, and practice studios rather than medium to large churches like St. Egbert’s. It should be noted that the size of the organ does not refer to the volume of the instrument, but rather to the number of manuals (keyboards), the number of stops (a stop in a pipe organ is a set of pipes that are identical in tonal quality and differ only in pitch), and other features that provide flexibilities and capabilities to the organist.

The stop selection on the current organ is disappointingly lacking in a few areas. First, there is no 8’ principal stop in the Swell division. Stops at the 8’ pitch form the foundation of the sound, as this is the most natural octave for the human voice, and principal stops, as the name implies, are the most important stops on the organ. Principal stops produce the characteristic “bright” sound that most people think of when they think of an organ. Principal chorus are used very heavily in liturgical worship as the primary means of supporting congregational hymn singing. The absence of an 8’ principal stop in the Swell division results in a hollow sound that lacks adequate foundation, and seems to be a design choice intended to compel customers to buy larger instruments. To make matters worse, the 4’ principal (Spitzprinzipal 4’) is described by Allen as “a hybrid stop which is predominately principal but with a string-like edge,” but regrettably it sounds like neither a principal nor a string stop. In fact, it is arguably the worst sounding stop on the organ, which leaves the Swell division with no principal chorus for hymn singing.

The organ also lacks reed stops, nasally stops that resemble read instruments such as oboes or clarinets. Reedss can be used in any number of ways, but two common ways are to “solo” out a melody line, meaning the melody sounds louder and crisper over the rest of the chords, making it easier to follow the song. This is particularly useful for teaching new songs or bringing a group of singers (choir or congregation) back together if they start fumbling with melody or tempo in the middle of a song. The only reed stop on this organ is Trompette 8’ stop in the Swell division. While this can be used as a solo stop for processional, recessional, and sometimes offertory hymns, a trumpet stop playing at communion, for example, is much less liturgically appropriate. A second way reed stops are used is to add a subtle crisp nasally edge to a principal or flute chorus, the former of which usually has a bright “ringy” sound and the latter a soft gentler sound. This can help the human ear hear the pitches being played and is often used in the first verse of a hymn to get people comfortable singing the melody. This technique requires a softer reed stop that is not overpowering. The Trompette 8’ does not meet this need.

Having multiple manuals makes it easy to quickly change the sound and/or volume of the organ in the middle of a piece. This is important for liturgical organists in the Catholic church. For example, responsorial psalms are often played using at least two manuals, one for the soft verse accompanying the cantor only, and another for stronger sound that brings in the congregational response. Additional manuals are handy when there are more combinations of musicians to support. For example, accompanying outside instruments (brass, reed, woodwind) requires a different registration than accompanying just a choir or a choir plus congregation. The current organ offers no good stop combinations that do not either completely overpower instruments on one hand or inadequately support singing on the other hand. Given the level of appreciation of music within St. Egbert’s parish, the robust congregational singing at most masses, the involvement of numerous instruments, choir members, and cantors, all with different musical abilities, the parish would benefit greatly from a larger instrument with at least three manuals and a dozen or so additional stops.
